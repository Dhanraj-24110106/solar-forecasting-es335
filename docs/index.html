Tomorrow's solar power, fifteen minutes at a time

A 21.5 MW plant in Surendranagar, Gujarat. One winter, 123 days, eight forecasting models. The question: can any of them beat simply saying tomorrow will be like today?

Dhanraj Visapara, 24110106
ES 335 Machine Learning course project
Source code on GitHub

Start here

A solar plant sells its electricity to the grid. The grid has to balance supply and demand at every moment, so the day before, each plant must tell the grid operator how much power it will produce tomorrow, in 96 slots of 15 minutes. This is a day-ahead forecast. Forecasts that miss by too much are a problem for the grid, and in Gujarat a forecast is counted as acceptable when it is within 7% of the plant's capacity.

The simplest forecast is to assume tomorrow will be like today: copy today's output, slot by slot. This project asks whether machine-learning models can do better, using the plant's own past output and weather measurements, and how much a forecast of tomorrow's weather would add.

The page follows that question in order. Setup describes the data, the models and how they are tested. Results compares the models and lets you inspect any day. Can we trust it? stress-tests the best result. The conclusion sums it up.

Capacity
The most the plant can deliver: 21.5 MW. All errors on this page are a percentage of it.
Block
One 15-minute slot of the day. Only daytime blocks, 07:00 to 18:15, are scored.
Sunlight (GHI)
How much sunlight reaches the ground, measured at the plant in W/m². The main driver of output.
Persistence
The tomorrow = today forecast. The baseline every model must beat.
Skill
How much smaller a model's error is than persistence's. Positive is better, zero is a tie, negative is worse.
Case 1, 2, 3
What a model is given: yesterday's output; plus yesterday's weather; plus tomorrow's actual weather.
Train, validation, test
The winter split by date. Models learn from train, settings are chosen on validation, and test is the final check.

What we found

  1. Typical error of tomorrow = today on the test week, as a share of capacity. A hard baseline to beat in a clear winter.
  2. Noneof the six models clearly beats it using only yesterday's power and weather (cases 1 and 2).
  3. Random Forest's error reduction on the test week once tomorrow's weather is known (case 3), the only clear win.

Setup

What is being forecast, from what data, with which models, and how they are scored. Everything below uses these words.

Question

On day D, forecast the power of each 15-minute block of day D+1. One row is one block; the target is its power. The forecast is made after day D ends. The claim covers one plant in one winter.

  • Case 1 power at the same block yesterday
  • Case 2 plus yesterday's sunlight, temperatures and wind
  • Case 3 plus tomorrow's measured weather, standing in for a perfect weather forecast: an upper bound, not a real forecast

Data

Plant SCADA and on-site weather station, 15 Oct 2025 to 14 Feb 2026: 11,808 rows, no gaps. Supplied by the course instructor and not redistributed; this page shows power only as a share of capacity.

Preprocessing

27 Nov had a block of data copied from another day: replaced by the average of 26 and 28 Nov. On 14 Jan the meter stuck at 12.80 MW: re-estimated from sunlight and module temperature. Night-time sunlight read 1.12 W/m²: set to 0. Both repaired days fall in training. Scalers for the penalised models are fitted on training data only.

Evaluation

Split by date, never shuffled, so models only learn from the past: train 92 days, validation 24, test 7. Every setting is chosen on validation; test is scored once. Only 07:00 to 18:15 counts, since nights are trivially zero. Errors are divided by capacity, not by actual power, which is near zero at dawn.

Skill = 1 − RMSE of the model ÷ RMSE of persistence. Zero means no better than tomorrow = today. Each skill has a 90% interval from resampling the days 1,000 times.

Why trees beat lines here

Power rises with sunlight, then levels off. Give a regression tree more leaves and it bends to follow the curve; the straight line cannot. Give it too many and it starts chasing noise.

Can we trust it?

Two checks on the results above: how much the best result depends on a perfect weather forecast, and what happens if the evaluation is done the wrong way. Then the limits of the claim.

How good must the weather forecast be?

Case 3 is told tomorrow's sunlight exactly. No real forecast is that good. Make the forecast worse and watch each model's lead over tomorrow = today shrink.

Always the case 3 models, on the days chosen with the switch above. Each day's sunlight is multiplied by a random factor (the same all day, since forecasts tend to be wrong for a whole day); skill is averaged over 30 random draws. The models are the case 3 models, unchanged.

Break the evaluation

The models are tested on days after the ones they learned from. Shuffle the data instead, so training and testing are mixed in time, and the same models suddenly look much better. They are not: they have seen the answer's neighbours.

Same models and settings; only the split changes. Uses the inputs chosen in the bar above, scored on the test week. Shuffled results are averaged over 5 shuffles. Part of the first jump is also that shuffled test days come from the whole winter, not the calm final week; the step from shuffled days to shuffled blocks isolates the leakage. None of these shuffled scores is a result to report.

Limitations

One plant, one winter. The test set is seven calm, clear days, so its intervals are wide. Case 3 uses measured weather; a real system would use weather forecasts with their own errors. Real schedules are submitted before day D ends. Wind direction is on an unknown scale. These are forecasts, not causes.

Conclusion

Yesterday's data cannot tell a model about tomorrow's clouds, so in a clear winter nothing reliably beats tomorrow = today. Knowing tomorrow's weather changes that, and a model that can follow the plant's midday plateau makes the most of it: Random Forest cuts the test error by about a fifth. That lead survives only while the sunlight forecast is accurate to within roughly 15 to 28%, depending on the period. The practical next step is to feed in real weather forecasts.
